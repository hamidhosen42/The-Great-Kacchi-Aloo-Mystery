# Related prior competitions: lessons for "The Great Kacchi Aloo Mystery"

Scope: prior Kaggle competitions that resemble Kacchi Aloo (synthetic, program-generated tabular data; binary target defined by a band on a ratio with noisy edges; 800 train / 400 test rows; ~181-row public LB; train-to-test covariate shift toward larger weddings; injected dirt).

How things were verified. Every link below was confirmed with WebFetch or WebSearch. Kaggle pages are JavaScript-rendered, so WebFetch only returns their page title; it does return 404 for competitions, notebooks and write-ups that don't exist. Discussion-post contents therefore come from WebSearch snippets or from third-party write-ups that I read. Anything I could not confirm is marked **UNVERIFIED**. Resource IDs (Cxxx) refer to `resources_competitions.csv`.

---

## Quick reference: how noisy a 181-row public LB is

These are my own calculations from the binomial formula, not taken from any source:

| True accuracy | 1 SE on 181 rows | Value of one row |
|---|---|---|
| 0.80 | ±3.0 pp | 0.55 pp |
| 0.90 | ±2.2 pp | 0.55 pp |
| 0.95 | ±1.6 pp | 0.55 pp |

So a public-LB "improvement" of 1–3 rows is indistinguishable from noise. Every competition below punished people who treated changes this small as real.

## 1. Instant Gratification (2019): reverse-engineering the generator wins

- **Link:** https://www.kaggle.com/c/instant-gratification (C001)
- **Year:** 2019. A synchronous Kernels-only competition, per a WebSearch snippet. Team repos report 1,836 teams (C006).
- **Data structure:** Synthetic. Generated by `sklearn.datasets.make_classification` with `n_clusters_per_class=3`, `flip_y=0.05`, `class_sep=1.0` and `n_informative` between 33 and 47. There were 512 sub-datasets, each identified by a discrete "magic" column (`wheezy-copper-turtle-magic`). Source: Ceshine Lee's post-mortem, https://blog.ceshine.net/post/kaggle-insta-grat/ (C005). Generator reference: https://scikit-learn.org/stable/modules/generated/sklearn.datasets.make_classification.html (C007).
- **Metric:** **UNVERIFIED.** The official page content could not be read.
- **What the top approaches did (verified):**
  - **1st place**, https://www.kaggle.com/c/instant-gratification/discussion/96549 (C002, via WebSearch). The winner ran a loop: QDA cross-validation predictions; then pseudo-label the test set and flip training labels that disagree; then fit a 6-cluster GMM per batch (3 clusters per class) and map clusters to classes; then repeat once. Reported CV 0.9754, public 0.9744, private 0.97598, with "minimal tuning beyond threshold selections". The author name "infinitezxc" appears in one snippet only (**UNVERIFIED** beyond that).
  - **Chris Deotte, "Pseudo Labeling - QDA - [0.969]"**, https://www.kaggle.com/code/cdeotte/pseudo-labeling-qda-0-969 (C003). One QDA model per magic value, plus pseudo-labeling.
  - **Chris Deotte, "3 Clusters Per Class - [0.975]"**, https://www.kaggle.com/cdeotte/3-clusters-per-class-0-975 (C004). Structuring the model to match the inferred generator parameter.
  - **Team repo (64th place)**, https://github.com/hakubishin3/kaggle_Instant_Gratification (C006). A GMM per class, then QDA on the cluster labels. The team identified `make_classification` from EDA.
  - The discussion that revealed the generating function call is linked from C005: https://www.kaggle.com/c/instant-gratification/discussion/96519. Its content is known only through that blog.
- **Validation lessons:** For the winner, CV, public and private scores agreed to within about 0.001. Once the model family matches the generator, CV becomes very reliable. The remaining error is essentially `flip_y` label noise, which is the Bayes floor.
- **Leakage / LB-probing story:** None was reported. The "magic" column was a deliberate structural hint, not a leak.
- **Transferable ideas for Kacchi Aloo:**
  1. Treat the task as **recovering the generator**. Plot P(target | aloo_count/guests) in fine bins. Estimate the two band edges (about 1 and about 2) and the width of the noisy edges. Fit a 2–4 parameter model, such as a logistic function of signed distance to the band.
  2. Estimate the **irreducible noise rate**, the analogue of `flip_y`, from rows far from the edges. This gives a realistic accuracy ceiling, and anything above it on the public LB is luck.
  3. Possibly **pseudo-label** the test rows. They are larger weddings, so adding them also exposes the model to the test size range. Gate this on CV run on an extrapolation fold.
  4. Handle noisy labels near the edges by down-weighting or flipping them, testing each variant inside CV.

## 2. Don't Overfit! II (2019): tiny data, and the public LB as a temptation

- **Link:** https://www.kaggle.com/c/dont-overfit-ii (C008)
- **Year:** 2019 (per the task brief; the competition page itself was not readable).
- **Data structure:** 250 training rows, 19,750 test rows, 300 continuous features, binary target. This comes from a WebSearch snippet of the Towards Data Science article (C010).
- **Metric:** AUC, per a WebSearch snippet.
- **What the top approaches did (verified):**
  - **2nd place, Chris Deotte, "LB Probing Strategies - [0.890] - 2nd Place"**, https://www.kaggle.com/code/cdeotte/lb-probing-strategies-0-890-2nd-place (C009). Per WebSearch snippets, he used leaderboard probes to estimate how useful each feature was (AUC_k − 0.5 as a proxy for its coefficient). He then fit an L1-penalised logistic regression on the training data combined with the public-test information. Coefficients with |w| < 0.04 were set to zero because they could not be trusted.
  - Towards Data Science write-up, https://towardsdatascience.com/dont-overfit-ii-how-to-avoid-overfitting-in-your-machine-learning-and-deep-learning-models-2ff903f4b36a/ (C010). With fewer rows than features, embedded (L1) selection and RFE worked best. The author notes that the top-3 contestants used LB probing.
  - **1st-place solution: UNVERIFIED** (not found).
- **Validation lessons:** With 250 rows, CV variance is huge. Sparse linear models and strong regularisation were what generalised.
- **Leakage / LB-probing story:** The public LB was exploited as a source of labels. That worked under this competition's setup, but it fits the public slice rather than the problem.
- **Transferable ideas for Kacchi Aloo:**
  1. 800 rows is also "tiny" for model selection. Use **repeated** stratified CV (for example 20×5-fold), report mean ± SE, and pick the **simplest model within 1 SE** of the best.
  2. Use L1 or permutation importance to confirm that only the ratio carries signal and the other columns are noise. Drop the noise columns rather than letting trees fit them.
  3. **Do not probe** the 181-row public LB. Check the Kacchi rules first (**UNVERIFIED** whether probing is allowed). Even where it is allowed, it only optimises the public slice.

## 3. Santander Customer Transaction Prediction (2019): synthetic artifacts as "magic"

- **Link:** https://www.kaggle.com/c/santander-customer-transaction-prediction (C014)
- **Year:** 2019
- **Data structure:** 200,000 training and 200,000 test rows, 200 anonymised numeric variables, about 10% positives. Source: the hackmd case study at https://hackmd.io/s/B1YMZu6wN.
- **Metric:** ROC AUC (per secondary sources; the official page was not readable).
- **What the top approaches did (verified):**
  - **#1 Solution**, https://www.kaggle.com/competitions/santander-customer-transaction-prediction/discussion/89003 (C015, via WebSearch). It used 400 engineered features. LightGBM with 600 features scored 0.92522 public / 0.92332 private, and a custom NN scored 0.92687 / 0.92546. The final blend was 2.1 × NN + 1 × LGBM and also used pseudo-labeling and data augmentation. The winner said most of the gain from 0.901 to 0.92x came from identifying the fake test data. The author name is **UNVERIFIED**.
  - **YaG320, "List of Fake Samples and Public/Private LB split"**, https://www.kaggle.com/yag320/list-of-fake-samples-and-public-private-lb-split (C016). About 100k test rows were synthetic. A row with at least one value that is unique in its column is real; otherwise it is fake.
  - **Corey Levinson, 13th place**, https://www.linkedin.com/pulse/winning-13th-place-kaggles-magic-competition-corey-levinson (C017).
    - Per-feature value-count features were the "magic".
    - After removing the fake rows, adversarial-validation AUC dropped from 99.999% to 50.2%.
    - The features were independent, so 200 per-feature models combined as a sum of logs reached LB 0.921.
    - In his words, "Standard feature engineering techniques...didn't work."
- **Validation lessons:** Adversarial validation exposed that the train/test mismatch was entirely due to the injected fake rows. Once they were removed, CV and LB agreed.
- **Leakage / LB-probing story:** The generator left fingerprints: synthetic rows reused existing values. That revealed the fake rows and even the public/private split.
- **Transferable ideas for Kacchi Aloo:**
  1. Run **adversarial validation** on the raw columns and then on the ratio alone. The expected result is that guests and aloo_count separate train from test (larger weddings in test) while the ratio does not. If that holds, a ratio-only model is shift-robust. That is the central argument for the final submission.
  2. Look for **generator fingerprints**: integer patterns, rounding, how aloo_count is drawn given guests, and how the dirt (missing values, Bangla digits, ×10 typos) is distributed. Check whether the dirt rates differ by target or between train and test. Dirt that correlates with the target is a signal; dirt that doesn't should simply be cleaned.
  3. Make per-feature univariate checks to confirm that only the ratio matters.

## 4. LANL Earthquake Prediction (2019): covariate shift and a huge shake-up

- **Link:** https://www.kaggle.com/c/LANL-Earthquake-Prediction (C025). 1st-place write-up: https://www.kaggle.com/competitions/LANL-Earthquake-Prediction/writeups/the-zoo-1st-place-solution (title verified).
- **Year:** 2019
- **Data structure:** Laboratory acoustic signal segments; the task was to predict time-to-failure.
- **Metric:** MAE. The public LB used 13% of the test set and the private LB 87%, per the PNAS paper (C027): https://www.pnas.org/doi/10.1073/pnas.2011362118.
- **What the top approaches did (verified):**
  - **1st place "The Zoo"**, as told by team member Corey Levinson at https://www.linkedin.com/pulse/my-team-won-20000-1st-place-kaggles-earthquake-corey-levinson (C026).
    - The test set contained **longer earthquake cycles than train**. Kolmogorov–Smirnov statistics showed that test features matched higher time-to-failure segments.
    - The team trained on 10 selected cycles (mean TTF 6.258) with shuffled 3-fold CV and averaged LightGBM and a multi-task NN with a geometric mean.
    - Its **two final submissions hedged the shift** (one biased toward higher TTF, one lower). Both would have won.
  - Per WebSearch snippets of PNAS and press coverage, the winners' best submission ranked **2000+ on the public LB**. The competition drew 4,521 teams.
- **Validation lessons:** Under covariate shift, random-split CV and the public LB were both misleading. The winners made their **training/validation data look like the test set**.
- **Leakage / LB-probing story:** No leak. The public LB gave actively misleading feedback, and teams that chased it fell on the private LB.
- **Transferable ideas for Kacchi Aloo:** This is the closest structural analogue to Kacchi's "test has larger weddings than train".
  1. Build an **extrapolation fold**: validate on the top guest-count quantile of train and train on the rest. A tree model on raw counts should degrade there, while a ratio model should not.
  2. **Reweight** the training data toward the test distribution of guests, using importance weights from the adversarial classifier or KS matching.
  3. **Hedge the two final submissions** on the shift assumption: (a) a pure ratio-band rule, which is scale-invariant; (b) the best shift-aware CV model.

## 5. Kaggle Tabular Playground / Playground Series: synthetic data, planted interactions, CV versus LB

- **Links:**
  - TPS Oct 2021 (CTGAN-generated): https://www.kaggle.com/c/tabular-playground-series-oct-2021 (C018)
  - TPS Nov 2021, AmbrosM "#3 Solution: Don't trust the cv scores": https://www.kaggle.com/competitions/tabular-playground-series-nov-2021/writeups/ambrosm-3-solution-don-t-trust-the-cv-scores (C024)
  - TPS Feb 2022 discussion listing, which contains AmbrosM's "#1 Solution: Exploiting the Flawed Random Generation": https://www.kaggle.com/c/tabular-playground-series-feb-2022/discussion/ (C019). The specific post URL is **UNVERIFIED**.
  - TPS May 2022 (planted feature interactions): https://www.kaggle.com/c/tabular-playground-series-may-2022 (C020). AmbrosM EDA notebook: https://www.kaggle.com/code/ambrosm/tpsmay22-eda-which-makes-sense
  - Playground S6E2, 1st place "Diversity, Selection, and Trusting the CV–LB Relation": https://www.kaggle.com/competitions/playground-series-s6e2/writeups/1st-place-solution-diversity-selection-and-t (C022)
  - Playground S6E2, 4th place "Why 'Less is More' (Stumps & Rank Ensembling)": https://www.kaggle.com/competitions/playground-series-s6e2/writeups/4th-place-solution (C023)
- **Years:** 2021–2022 for the TPS episodes. The S6E2 year is **UNVERIFIED** (Season 6).
- **Data structure:** Synthetic tabular data made by a learned generator from a real dataset: CTGAN for TPS Oct 2021, a deep-learning generator for the later Playground Series (C021). TPS May 2022 explicitly "includes a number of different feature interactions" (quoted in a participant's README).
- **Metric:** Varies; TPS Oct 2021 used ROC AUC.
- **What the top approaches did (verified):**
  - **TPS Feb 2022, 1st place:** the post title says the win came from *exploiting the flawed random generation*. Details are **UNVERIFIED**.
  - **TPS May 2022:** per a WebSearch snippet, EDA of 2-D projections (for example f_05 against f_22) produced hand-made interaction features such as a ternary `i_05_22`. The attribution to a specific author is **UNVERIFIED**.
  - **TPS Nov 2021, AmbrosM, 3rd place:** train and test lay in different regions of feature space and the data were chunked. GroupKFold CV became useless, so he relied on the public LB instead. This is a deliberate counterpoint to "always trust CV".
  - **S6E2, 1st place:** about 150 OOF predictions, an Optuna subset search and a Ridge stacker. The author did **not** pick the highest-CV submission because the CV–LB relation degraded above that level. The final scored CV 0.9557801, public 0.95396, private 0.95535.
  - **S6E2, 4th place:** the signal was nearly linear and the generator "heavily penalized over-engineering". Plain logistic regression was strong, and the final used depth-2 trees with rank averaging (private 0.95534).
- **Validation lessons:**
  - CV is trustworthy only when the folds mimic the train-to-test relationship (TPS Nov 2021).
  - Pick finals from a stable CV plateau and sanity-check the CV–LB relationship (S6E2, 1st).
  - Simple models win when the generator is simple (S6E2, 4th).
- **Leakage / LB-probing story:** Generator flaws were exploited legitimately in TPS Feb 2022. No probing stories were verified.
- **Transferable ideas for Kacchi Aloo:**
  1. **Engineer the interaction explicitly**: the ratio, an in-band flag, and the signed distance to the nearest edge. This replaces what a GBM would otherwise approximate with axis-aligned splits on raw counts, which cannot extrapolate to larger weddings.
  2. **Prefer depth-1/2 models or logistic models** on these features. Only accept added complexity if it beats the simple model by more than 1 SE.
  3. Follow the AmbrosM nuance: if adversarial validation shows strong shift, plain KFold CV is not enough. But Kacchi's public LB has only 181 rows, too few to trust instead, so **build the shift into the CV** (extrapolation fold, reweighting) rather than switching to the LB.

## 6. Titanic: Machine Learning from Disaster (what to avoid)

- **Link:** https://www.kaggle.com/competitions/titanic (C011)
- **Year:** a long-running Getting Started competition; the start year was not confirmed.
- **Data structure:** A small real passenger table with a binary survival target. Exact row counts were not confirmed from a page I read.
- **Metric:** Accuracy, the same as Kacchi.
- **What top approaches did / stories (verified):**
  - Dataquest, https://www.dataquest.io/blog/kaggle-fundamentals/ (C013): "anyone scoring about 90% on this competition is likely cheating (it's easy to look up the names of the passengers in the holdout set online and see if they survived)." The visible 100% scores are therefore not skill.
  - David Recio, "The Titanic has a Leak", https://david-recio.com/2022/04/11/titanic-leak.html (C012). Families split across train and test leak survival information. With leak-proof grouped CV, XGBoost scored 0.822 against 0.801 for an enhanced gender baseline. A group-survival model scored 0.829 normally but only 0.780 leak-proof. Most differences were **1–2 standard errors**.
- **Validation lessons:** On small accuracy-scored test sets, most leaderboard differences are within noise. Group structure has to be respected in CV.
- **Leakage / LB-probing story:** Labels were looked up externally, and families leaked across the train/test split.
- **Transferable ideas for Kacchi Aloo:**
  1. Integrity guardrail: no external label lookups and no LB probing unless the rules allow it.
  2. Check whether Kacchi rows share hidden groups, such as repeated wedding, venue or date patterns. Use GroupKFold if so.
  3. Always report CV ± SE and treat differences under 1 SE as ties.

## Also relevant: shake-up case studies on small or noisy public sets

- **Mercedes-Benz Greener Manufacturing (2017)**, https://www.kaggle.com/c/mercedes-benz-greener-manufacturing (C028). The metric was R². Per a WebSearch snippet (probably from https://www.kaggle.com/dmi3kno/mercedesbenz-competition-leaderboard-shakeup), the shake-up was 0.1462 overall, 0.2398 for the top 10% and 0.3082 for the top 1%. The biggest rise was 3,808 places and the biggest fall 3,564. **The higher the public rank, the bigger the shake-up.**
- **Restaurant Revenue Prediction (TFI)**, https://www.kaggle.com/c/restaurant-revenue-prediction (C029). It had 137 training rows and 100,000 test rows, of which only about 321 were real and scored (https://github.com/bensolucky/TFI). The "Our perfect submission" thread (https://news.ycombinator.com/item?id=9659208, C030) describes a team that hill-climbed to #1 on the public LB and then fell about 2,000 places on the private LB.
- **Theory:**
  - Moritz Hardt, "Competing in a data science contest without reading the data" (http://blog.mrtz.org/2015/03/09/competition.html, C033), and the Ladder paper (http://arxiv.org/abs/1502.04585).
  - Roelofs et al., NeurIPS 2019 meta-analysis (https://papers.nips.cc/paper/9117-a-meta-analysis-of-overfitting-in-machine-learning, C034). It found little adaptive overfitting across 100+ competitions. My interpretation: at n=181, sampling noise rather than adaptivity is the main danger.

## Consolidated transferable plan for Kacchi Aloo

1. Clean the dirt deterministically: Bangla digits to ASCII, ×10 typo repair, missing values. Put any thresholds learned from the data inside the CV folds.
2. Engineer the ratio, an in-band flag and the signed distance to the edges.
3. Run adversarial validation on the raw columns and on the ratio, expecting a raw-count shift and no ratio shift.
4. Use a shift-aware CV: repeated stratified KFold plus an extrapolation fold of the largest weddings, plus adversarial-weighted folds.
5. Compare models: a 2–4 parameter band rule, logistic regression on the band features, depth-2 GBM, and optionally TabPFN (C044/C045). Pick the simplest model within 1 SE.
6. Simulate 181/219 public/private splits on OOF predictions to estimate rank volatility.
7. Final pair: (a) the band rule or minimal model; (b) the best shift-aware CV model. Select both manually; Kaggle docs (C039) say auto-selection otherwise uses the best public scores.
